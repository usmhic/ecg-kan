  \textbf{\color{deepblue}Abstract.}\quad
  Because it reflects the electrical behaviour of a beating heart, the
  electrocardiogram (ECG) carries person-specific information that cannot be
  lifted from a photograph or a surface, which makes it an appealing
  biometric signal. We describe a verification pipeline that turns
  individual heartbeats into compact descriptors by applying the Wavelet
  Transform Modulus Maxima (WTMM) method to a Morlet continuous wavelet
  transform (CWT) scalogram. From each beat we build two representations: the
  z-scored raw waveform (shown in 2-D with t-SNE) and a 26-dimensional WTMM
  summary that records the number of ridge lines together with the mean and
  spread of peak frequency and peak power inside five log-spaced frequency
  bands. Each subject is treated as a separate one-versus-rest problem, and
  six one-class detectors are compared: One-Class SVM (OC-SVM), Isolation
  Forest, Local Outlier Factor (LOF), Elliptic Envelope, an MLP autoencoder,
  and a Kolmogorov--Arnold Network (KAN) autoencoder whose edges carry
  learnable B-spline functions. To our knowledge this is the first time a KAN
  autoencoder has been used for one-class ECG biometric verification. All
  experiments run on the ECG-ID database~\cite{r10} with 10 subjects, and we
  outline how the same pipeline transfers to other PhysioNet collections.
  Code and notebooks accompany the paper at \url{\repourl}~\cite{r1}.\\[4pt]
  \textbf{\color{deepblue}Keywords:}~
  ECG biometrics; wavelet transform modulus maxima; Morlet CWT;
  one-class classification; One-Class SVM; Isolation Forest; LOF;
  Kolmogorov--Arnold networks; B-spline autoencoder; anomaly detection;
  t-SNE visualisation.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Introduction}

Faces, fingerprints, and iris scans dominate everyday identification, yet
each of them can be lifted from a photograph or a touched surface without the
owner ever noticing. An ECG does not share this weakness: it measures the
electrical activity of the heart, a signal that is tied to an individual's
cardiac geometry and only exists while the heart is actually beating. That
built-in sign of life is part of why ECG-based verification keeps drawing
attention~\cite{r4}.

The harder question is how to represent the signal. A single heartbeat mixes
information across time and frequency simultaneously: the slow P and T waves
live mostly below 10\,Hz, whereas the sharp QRS complex occupies roughly
5--60\,Hz~\cite{r21}. Collapsing the beat into a single spectrum throws away
its timing, while keeping only the time-domain trace discards the frequency
picture. The Morlet CWT keeps both, because its analysis window shrinks at
high frequencies---sharpening the QRS in time---and stretches at low
frequencies, resolving the P and T waves in frequency~\cite{r14}. From the
CWT modulus, the WTMM skeleton~\cite{r5} retains only the ridge structures
that follow each beat's strongest singularities, discarding the rest of the
energy.

The majority of published ECG biometric systems are closed-set: they train
on every enrolled subject together~\cite{r2,r3}. Such a design is impossible
to use at enrolment, when only one person's recording exists. We take the
opposite stance. Each detector is trained on a single person's beats and, at
test time, decides beat by beat whether a new sample belongs to that person
or to an impostor. This is one-class (equivalently, open-set) verification.

Six detectors span the range from classical to neural. Four classical
one-class methods---OC-SVM~\cite{r6}, Isolation Forest~\cite{r7},
LOF~\cite{r8}, and Elliptic Envelope---serve as baselines, and an MLP
autoencoder is included as a conventional deep-learning reference. The novel
piece is a \textbf{KAN autoencoder}~\cite{r9}, in which every ordinary
weight-times-activation link is replaced by a learnable B-spline placed on the
edge. KANs have lately performed well on cardiac problems~\cite{r17,r18}, but
they have not been tried on one-class anomaly detection for biometrics.

Our reason for reaching for a KAN is tied to the data. WTMM feature vectors
are sparse: most entries hover near zero, and the useful signal is packed into
a handful of non-zero components. A standard MLP applies the same activation
shape everywhere, whereas a B-spline can put its degrees of freedom precisely
where the samples actually fall. Whether that flexibility pays off when only
15--30 beats per person are available is the empirical question this paper
sets out to answer.

Everything reported here can be reproduced from two companion Jupyter
notebooks~\cite{r1}: one covering the signal-processing chain and one covering
the classifier benchmark.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{ECG Biometrics}

An early systematic look at ECG identification came from
Israel~et~al.~\cite{r16}, who built features from PQRST intervals and
amplitudes and reported identification above 90\% on small cohorts. The QRS
detector of Pan and Tompkins~\cite{r13} remains the backbone of most
beat-segmentation pipelines, including the NeuroKit2 implementation~\cite{r12}
we rely on here.

Image-based approaches gained ground with Byeon~et~al.~\cite{r14}, who fed
Morlet scalograms of ECG segments into convolutional networks and showed that
the time-frequency image exposes between-subject cues that the raw trace
hides. Ozaltin and Yeniay~\cite{r15} paired CWT scalograms with a CNN-SVM
front end and reached up to 99.21\% on ECG-ID, though under closed-set
identification rather than one-class verification.

El~Boujnouni~et~al.~\cite{r2} combined CWT, DWT, and a capsule network across
four PhysioNet databases (ECG-ID among them) and reached as high as 100\%
identification. Their earlier study~\cite{r3} first established scalograms as
viable capsule-network inputs, which is the observation that motivates the
scalogram-to-feature route taken here. Both works, however, remain closed-set
and need every class at training time, in contrast to the open-set setting we
study.

\subsection{Wavelet Transform Modulus Maxima}

WTMM was introduced by Mallat and Hwang~\cite{r5} as a way to locate and
describe singularities across scale. Tracking the local maxima of the CWT
modulus toward finer scales pinpoints singularity locations, while their rate
of decay encodes the Lipschitz regularity. WTMM has already served ECG
denoising and beat classification~\cite{r21}, but using it as a feature
representation for one-class biometric verification is new.

\subsection{One-Class Classification}

OC-SVM~\cite{r6} separates the training points from the origin in a kernel
space with a maximum-margin boundary. Isolation Forest~\cite{r7} scores a
point by how few random splits are needed to isolate it. LOF~\cite{r8}
compares a point's local density to that of its neighbours. Autoencoder-based
detection~\cite{r22} learns to reconstruct normal data and flags samples whose
reconstruction error is large at test time; this idea has been used on ECG in
clinical work~\cite{r23}, but not with a KAN backbone aimed at biometric
verification.

\subsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold Networks}

Liu~et~al.~\cite{r9} proposed KANs as a drop-in replacement for MLP layers: an
independent B-spline sits on every edge instead of a fixed activation at every
node. On cardiac problems---heart-disease detection~\cite{r17} and arrhythmia
classification~\cite{r18}---KANs have matched or beaten conventional networks.
Their behaviour in one-class settings, trained on positive examples only, has
not yet been examined.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Primary Dataset: ECG-ID}

Our experiments use the \textbf{ECG-ID database}~\cite{r10}, hosted on
PhysioNet~\cite{r11}. It holds 310 single-lead resting recordings from 90
volunteers, digitised at $f_s = 500$\,Hz on ECG lead~I. Each session runs for
about 20\,s and contains 15--30 heartbeat cycles. Where a volunteer recorded
several sessions we keep only the first, following
El~Boujnouni~et~al.~\cite{r2}, who also worked with ECG-ID. We evaluate the
first 10 subjects, yielding 10 independent one-class problems.

\noindent
Ten subjects is a deliberate scope for this first study. Nothing in the code
assumes a particular subject count, so extending to all 90 subjects requires
no changes.

\subsection{Additional PhysioNet Datasets for Future Work}

ECG-ID fits single-session experiments well, but a few other PhysioNet
collections would sharpen a follow-up study by adding properties ECG-ID lacks.

\textbf{PTB-XL}~\cite{r26} offers 21{,}837 clinical 12-lead recordings from
18{,}885 patients at 500\,Hz. Its scale and multi-lead layout would show
whether WTMM features hold up on a much larger population and whether
agreement across leads adds discriminative power.

\textbf{MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm Database} provides 24-hour ambulatory
recordings from 18 subjects at 128\,Hz. It is a natural way to probe temporal
stability: does a WTMM vector taken in the morning still match the same person
in the afternoon?

\textbf{CYBHi Dataset}~\cite{r11} (reachable through PhysioNet) was built for
biometric evaluation and includes resting and post-exercise sessions for 65
subjects. The exercise recordings inject heart-rate variation and artefacts,
making them a stress test for any ECG biometric method.

\textbf{LUDB (Lobachevsky University Electrocardiography
Database)}~\cite{r27} supplies 200 12-lead recordings with expert
annotations of every wave (P, QRS, T), which would allow WTMM-derived
singularity positions to be checked directly against labelled wave boundaries.

Combining these with ECG-ID would make cross-database generalisation testable:
whether a model enrolled on one database can accept subjects from another
without re-fitting the feature extractor.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

The pipeline runs in five stages, split across two companion
notebooks~\cite{r1}: preprocessing, R-peak detection and beat segmentation,
the Morlet CWT, WTMM ridge extraction and feature computation, and finally
classifier training and evaluation. The remainder of this section walks
through each stage.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Signal Preprocessing}
\label{subsec:preproc}

Let $x[n]$ be the raw ECG at $f_s = 500$\,Hz. The two noise sources that
matter most are baseline wander (sub-0.5\,Hz drift from breathing and
electrode motion) and muscle artefact above 40\,Hz~\cite{r21}. In the
notebooks the cleaning is left to NeuroKit2~\cite{r12}, which runs a
fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth bandpass:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  x_{\text{clean}}[n]
  = \mathcal{F}_{\text{BP}}\!\left\{x[n]\right\},
  \qquad
  f_{\text{low}} = 0.5\,\text{Hz},
  \quad
  f_{\text{high}} = 40\,\text{Hz}.
  \label{eq:bp}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
Running the filter in both directions (\texttt{filtfilt}) removes any phase
shift. That matters here because a displaced R-peak would misalign every
window-based feature computed downstream.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{R-Peak Detection and Beat Segmentation}
\label{subsec:beats}

R-peaks come from NeuroKit2's Pan--Tompkins implementation~\cite{r12,r13},
which chains a derivative filter, a squaring step, and a moving-window
integrator before thresholding. Writing $\{r_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ for the detected
peaks, each beat $b_k$ is cut as:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  b_k[m]
  = x_{\text{clean}}\!\left[r_k + m\right],
  \qquad
  m \in \bigl[-T_{\text{pre}},\; T_{\text{post}}\bigr),
  \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
with $T_{\text{pre}} = 125$ samples (250\,ms) and $T_{\text{post}} = 225$
samples (450\,ms), i.e.\ a 700\,ms window of $N = 350$ samples. The window is
deliberately asymmetric: at resting rates the T wave finishes about
400--450\,ms after the R-peak~\cite{r21}, so more room is left on the right.
Beats whose window would spill past the recording are dropped. In the
notebooks the cut is done by NeuroKit2's \texttt{epochs\_create}, and each
beat is z-scored to cancel amplitude differences caused by electrode
placement~\cite{r2}:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{b}_k[m]
  = \frac{b_k[m] - \mu_{b_k}}{\sigma_{b_k} + \epsilon},
  \qquad \epsilon = 10^{-8}.
  \label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Morlet CWT and Scalogram}
\label{subsec:cwt}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

The ECG is non-stationary: P-wave energy sits below 10\,Hz, the QRS spans
5--60\,Hz, and the T wave again falls below 10\,Hz~\cite{r14,r21}. A global
spectrum merges these events, and a short-time Fourier transform is stuck with
one window width for all of them. The CWT instead adapts its window to scale:
narrow at high frequency for crisp QRS timing, wide at low frequency for clean
P- and T-wave spectra. This multi-resolution match to the cardiac cycle is
exactly what Byeon~et~al.~\cite{r14} and El~Boujnouni~et~al.~\cite{r2}
exploited when feeding Morlet scalograms to deep networks.

\subsubsection{CWT Definition}

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \CWT_{b_k}(a,\, \tau)
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
    b_k(t)\;
    \psi^*\!\!\left(\frac{t - \tau}{a}\right) dt,
  \label{eq:cwt}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
where $a > 0$ is the scale, $\tau$ the shift, and $1/\sqrt{a}$ keeps energy
comparable across scales. In the notebooks \texttt{pywt.cwt} evaluates this on
the discrete beat.

\subsubsection{Morlet Wavelet}

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\text{Morl}}(t)
  = \pi^{-1/4}\,
    e^{\,i\,\omega_0 t}\,
    e^{-t^2/2},
  \qquad \omega_0 = 6.
  \label{eq:morlet}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
The Gaussian envelope reaches the Heisenberg limit
$\Delta t \cdot \Delta \omega \geq \tfrac{1}{2}$, giving the best possible
joint time-frequency concentration. Fixing $\omega_0 = 6$ makes the
admissibility condition $\int \psi\,dt \approx 0$ hold numerically, so the
transform is invertible~\cite{r20}. PyWavelets returns the central frequency
via \texttt{cf = pywt.central\_frequency('morl')}, and scales are read off a
log-spaced frequency grid:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  a_j = \frac{c_\psi \cdot f_s}{f_j},
  \qquad f_j \in \bigl[\,1,\; 60\,\bigr]\,\text{Hz},
  \quad j = 1,\ldots,80.
  \label{eq:scale_freq}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\subsubsection{Scalogram}

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \Scalo(a,\, \tau)
  = \abs{\CWT_{b_k}(a,\, \tau)}^2.
  \label{eq:scalogram}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
Each beat therefore becomes an $80 \times 350$ matrix.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{WTMM Feature Extraction}
\label{subsec:wtmm}

\subsubsection{Ridge-Line Detection}

The theoretical footing is the singularity theorem of Mallat and
Hwang~\cite{r5,r20}: if $b_k$ has a singularity of Lipschitz exponent $\alpha$
at $t_0$, then $|\CWT_{b_k}(a,\tau)| \leq A\,a^{\alpha+1/2}$ as $a \to 0$ and
$\tau \to t_0$. The modulus maxima thus drift toward singularity locations as
the scale falls, and how fast they decay reveals $\alpha$. In an ECG the sharp
QRS onset has small $\alpha$ and produces strong maxima lines that survive
across all 80 scales, whereas the smoother P and T waves give weaker, shorter
lines.

A local maximum at $(a, \tau_0)$ satisfies:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial \tau}
  \abs{\CWT_{b_k}(a,\tau)}\bigg|_{\tau=\tau_0} = 0
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \abs{\CWT_{b_k}(a,\tau_0)}
  > \abs{\CWT_{b_k}(a,\tau)}
  \;\;\forall\,\tau \in \mathcal{N}(\tau_0).
  \label{eq:wtmm_def}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
On the discrete $80 \times 350$ grid this becomes a simple neighbourhood
test, implemented as \texttt{compute\_wtmm\_lines} in the notebook.

\subsubsection{Chain Linking}

Maxima on neighbouring scales are joined into chains when:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \abs{\tau_0^{(a+\delta a)} - \tau_0^{(a)}}
  \;\leq\;
  \lambda(a)
  = \max\!\left(1,\;\left\lfloor\tfrac{a}{2}\right\rfloor\right).
  \label{eq:link}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
The tolerance $\lambda(a)$ grows with scale, mirroring the wavelet's widening
support. Chains that span fewer than two consecutive scale rows are treated as
noise and dropped.

\subsubsection{WTMM Statistical Feature Vector}

Instead of a fixed-size pixel image---which would need enough beats to cover
its full dimensionality---the notebooks compress the chain set into a compact
26-dimensional vector. The choice is driven by the scarcity of beats per
subject (15--30) and follows the summary-statistic philosophy of~\cite{r21}.

Let $L$ be the number of chains found in beat $b_k$. The frequency axis
$[f_{\min}, f_{\max}]$ is split into $N_B = 5$ log-spaced bands
$[f_\ell^{\text{lo}},\, f_\ell^{\text{hi}}]$, $\ell = 1,\ldots,5$. For each
band the code gathers every chain point whose frequency
$f_j = c_\psi f_s / a_j$ lands inside it. A non-empty band yields five numbers:
its line count $C_\ell$, the mean peak frequency $\bar{f}_\ell$, the frequency
spread $\sigma_{f,\ell}$, the mean peak power $\bar{p}_\ell$, and the power
spread $\sigma_{p,\ell}$; an empty band contributes five zeros. Stacking the
overall count with the per-band blocks gives:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}(b_k)
  = \Bigl[
      L,\;
      C_1,\, \bar{f}_1,\, \sigma_{f,1},\, \bar{p}_1,\, \sigma_{p,1},\;
      \ldots,\;
      C_5,\, \bar{f}_5,\, \sigma_{f,5},\, \bar{p}_5,\, \sigma_{p,5}
    \Bigr]
  \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{26}.
  \label{eq:feat_vec}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
As a second feature set, every detector also receives the z-scored raw
waveform $\tilde{b}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{350}$. For \emph{visualisation}
(Figure~\ref{fig:tsne_space}) we project both feature sets to 2-D with
t-SNE~\cite{r25}, which emphasises local neighbourhood structure and therefore
makes it easy to see whether different people's beats fall into separate
clusters. For \emph{training}, the raw waveform is compressed to 40 principal
components (PCA) fitted on each subject's training split; t-SNE is reserved for
the 2-D scatter plots, since it is stochastic at small sample sizes and cannot
be inverted cleanly. The PCA dimensionality is clipped to
$\min(n_{\text{train}},\, 350,\, 40)$ so that subjects with very few beats do
not trigger rank-deficiency errors.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Traditional One-Class Classifiers}
\label{subsec:trad}

\subsubsection{One-Class SVM}

Every vector $\mathbf{x}_i$ is lifted into an RBF kernel space
$k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \exp(-\gamma\norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'}^2)$ with
$\gamma = 1/d$ (the \texttt{scale} default, $d$ being the feature dimension).
OC-SVM~\cite{r6} then solves:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\xi},\rho}
  \;\frac{1}{2}\norm{\mathbf{w}}^2
  - \rho
  + \frac{1}{\nu n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_i
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
  \langle\mathbf{w},\phi(\mathbf{x}_i)\rangle \geq \rho - \xi_i,
  \;\xi_i \geq 0,
  \label{eq:ocsvm}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
with $\nu = 0.1$. A test beat is accepted when its decision value is positive.

\subsubsection{Isolation Forest}

Isolation Forest~\cite{r7} grows 200 random trees. Anomalies are isolated with
fewer splits, so the mean path length over the ensemble becomes the anomaly
score. Contamination is set to 0.1 in the notebooks.

\subsubsection{Local Outlier Factor}

LOF~\cite{r8} compares the local reachability density around a point with that
of its neighbours; a ratio near 1 marks an inlier, and a ratio well above 1
marks an outlier. The neighbour count is clamped to
$\min(20,\, n_{\text{train}} - 1)$ so the estimator does not break when a
subject has fewer beats than the default $k = 20$.

\subsubsection{Elliptic Envelope}

Elliptic Envelope fits a Gaussian to the training beats and scores samples by
their Mahalanobis distance from the centre. Its assumption of a roughly
elliptical genuine class is questionable for the high-dimensional raw waveform
but is reasonable for the 26-dimensional WTMM summary.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{MLP Autoencoder}
\label{subsec:mlp}

The MLP autoencoder learns to rebuild each genuine beat from a narrow
bottleneck, so that impostor beats---never seen in training---incur larger
reconstruction error at test time. Its architecture is:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \text{Encoder:}\;
  d \xrightarrow{128} \xrightarrow{64} 16,
  \qquad
  \text{Decoder:}\;
  16 \xrightarrow{64} \xrightarrow{128} d,
  \label{eq:mlp_arch}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
with ReLU activations throughout. Training uses Adam~\cite{r24} with
$\eta = 10^{-3}$, batch size 32, and 80 epochs. The anomaly score is the
negative mean squared reconstruction error,
$s(\mathbf{x}) = -\norm{\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}}^2$, and the accept
threshold is placed at the 90th percentile of training errors, so 10\% of
genuine training beats are treated as borderline.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{KAN Autoencoder}
\label{subsec:kan}

\subsubsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold Representation}

The Kolmogorov--Arnold theorem states that any continuous
$f:[0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ can be written as:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  f(x_1,\dots,x_n)
  = \sum_{q=0}^{2n}
    \Phi_q\!\left(
      \sum_{p=1}^{n} \phi_{q,p}(x_p)
    \right),
  \label{eq:ka_theorem}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
so that all of the complexity is carried by univariate functions. A
KAN~\cite{r9} realises this by attaching a learnable univariate function to
every edge of the network.

\subsubsection{KAN Layer}

The edge running from neuron $j$ to neuron $i$ computes:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{ij}(x)
  = w_b\;\mathrm{silu}(x)
  + w_s\;\sum_{k} c_{ijk}\, B_k(x),
  \label{eq:kan_edge}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
where $\mathrm{silu}(x) = x/(1+e^{-x})$ is a fixed residual base, the $B_k$ are
cubic B-spline bases on a uniform grid of $G = 5$ intervals over $[-1, 1]$
(order $k = 3$, hence $G + k = 8$ bases per edge), and $w_b$, $w_s$, and
$\{c_{ijk}\}$ are learned. Inputs are clamped to $[-1, 1]$ at the forward pass
to keep the splines inside their support. Neuron $i$ in layer $\ell+1$ then
outputs $x_i^{(\ell+1)} = \sum_{j} \phi_{ij}(x_j^{(\ell)})$.

Because each B-spline is locally supported, the model concentrates its
flexibility where the training data actually sits---an appealing property for
WTMM features, most of whose entries cluster near zero.

\subsubsection{Architecture and Training}

The KAN autoencoder keeps the same depth as the MLP:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \text{Encoder:}\;
  d \xrightarrow{\phi^{(1)}} 64 \xrightarrow{\phi^{(2)}} 12,
  \qquad
  \text{Decoder:}\;
  12 \xrightarrow{\phi^{(3)}} 64 \xrightarrow{\phi^{(4)}} d.
  \label{eq:kan_arch}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
Every arrow is one \texttt{BSplineEdge} layer from the companion notebook.
Training uses AdamW~\cite{r24} with $\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay
$10^{-4}$, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, a cosine-annealing schedule, batch
size 32, and 80 epochs. The loss is mean squared error, and anomaly scoring
and thresholding follow the same recipe as the MLP autoencoder.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:eval}

For each of the 10 subjects, 70\,\% of their beats train the model and the
remaining 30\,\% form the genuine test set. An equal number of beats drawn
uniformly at random from the other subjects makes up the impostor test set,
which balances the binary split. The random seed changes per subject
($42 + \text{subject\_index}$) so that no single seed choice drives the
results.

All six detectors are trained and tested independently for every subject,
giving 10 one-class problems, and each problem is run on both feature sets (raw
PCA-40 and WTMM-26). We report:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{AUROC}: area under the ROC curve, a threshold-free measure of
    how well genuine and impostor scores separate; 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is
    perfect.
  \item \textbf{F1 score}: the harmonic mean of precision and recall at the
    training-percentile threshold.
  \item \textbf{EER}: the equal-error rate, i.e.\ the operating point where the
    false-rejection and false-acceptance rates coincide; lower is better.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Each metric is summarised as a mean and standard deviation over the 10
subjects. All figures and the results table are produced automatically by the
consolidated companion script \texttt{run\_ecg\_pipeline.py}, which uses t-SNE
for Figure~\ref{fig:tsne_space}. The full code lives in the companion
notebooks~\cite{r1}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{WTMM as a One-Class Feature Representation}

Two properties make the WTMM summary a good fit for one-class verification.
The first is \emph{within-subject consistency}: the major cardiac events---the
QRS onset and the T-wave peak---land in stable positions from beat to beat, so
a single subject's 26-dimensional vectors bunch into a tight cluster
(Figure~\ref{fig:tsne_space}). The second is \emph{between-subject
discriminability}: differences in cardiac anatomy translate into different
chain counts, frequency distributions, and per-band energy profiles. Both are
needed at once---a tight genuine cluster keeps the boundary learnable from only
15--30 beats, while discriminability keeps impostors on the far side of it.
This echoes El~Boujnouni~et~al.~\cite{r2}, who showed that Morlet scalograms
expose between-subject cues absent from the raw signal.

The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:results} bear this out. WTMM lifts the mean
AUROC above the raw waveform for every model but Elliptic Envelope, and it
produces the two strongest AUROC results overall (Isolation Forest 0.932,
OC-SVM 0.902). The KAN autoencoder also reaches its best EER on WTMM (0.113),
the lowest of any model on either feature set. In short, the compact 26-d
summary keeps the structure the models need while shedding the beat-to-beat
amplitude noise that inflates errors on the raw waveform.

\subsection{Behaviour of Traditional Classifiers}

On raw PCA-40 features, Elliptic Envelope tops the AUROC ranking (0.898),
which suggests the genuine beats form a roughly Gaussian blob in the first 40
PCA directions. Isolation Forest (0.860) and OC-SVM (0.823) trail closely, and
Isolation Forest also posts the lowest raw-feature EER (0.136), i.e.\ the most
balanced threshold. LOF (0.651) clearly lags, in part because clamping its
neighbourhood at 15--30 samples weakens its density estimates---LOF works best
when many neighbours are on hand.

On WTMM features, Isolation Forest leads with room to spare (AUROC 0.932, EER
0.134), OC-SVM just behind (0.902, EER 0.132). Elliptic Envelope slips from
0.898 to 0.892, a small but consistent drop confirming that the 26-dimensional
WTMM space is less Gaussian than the PCA-compressed waveform. LOF climbs from
0.651 to 0.837 in AUROC but collapses to an F1 of 0.000, a threshold-calibration
failure on the compact space. That Isolation Forest thrives on WTMM fits its
tree logic: each feature (line count, band energy, and so on) is a
semi-independent summary, so random axis-aligned cuts isolate outliers
naturally.

\subsection{MLP vs.\ KAN Autoencoder}

On the raw waveform, both autoencoders trail the best traditional model---the
MLP at AUROC 0.878 / EER 0.168 and the KAN at 0.759 / 0.260. With 350 values
compressed to 40 by PCA, the input is still high-dimensional relative to the
training data, and that hurts the KAN more. Its B-spline edges need enough
coverage to shape their knots, and with only 10--20 beats after the split the
splines appear to under-fit the raw waveform's spread.

The compact WTMM features flip the picture. The KAN (AUROC 0.886) lands within
0.046 of Isolation Forest (0.932) and edges out the MLP (0.874). More telling,
the KAN reaches the lowest EER of the whole study, 0.113, against 0.151 for the
MLP and 0.132 for Isolation Forest. Since EER is often the most operationally
meaningful figure---it is the balanced false-accept / false-reject
point---this advantage matters. It seems to arise because the B-splines pack
their flexibility into the sparse near-zero region where most WTMM values sit,
yielding a better-calibrated reconstruction threshold.

The F1 gap between the two (0.510 for the MLP vs.\ 0.421 for the KAN on WTMM)
points the other way, and it is a threshold effect: the MLP's fixed
90th-percentile rule happens to match the test distribution slightly better
for this metric. A per-subject threshold search would probably close the gap.
The KAN's latent geometry, its sensitivity to the number of B-spline knots, and
its scaling to more subjects all remain open and are left to future work.

\subsection{Comparison with Closed-Set Methods}

One-class verification is inherently harder than the closed-set identification
of El~Boujnouni~et~al.~\cite{r2,r3}, where a model sees every subject during
training and can lean on inter-class boundaries; our models see only one
person at enrolment. A direct accuracy comparison would be unfair, but the
per-subject AUROC heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}) shows a familiar pattern:
subjects whose beats cluster tightly in the t-SNE space (Persons~02, 03, 10 in
Figure~\ref{fig:tsne_space}) are easy for every model, while subjects with
diffuse clusters (Person~07, whose WTMM AUROC drops to 0.537 for LOF) stay hard
no matter which model is used. The bottleneck, then, is the signal
itself---likely irregular rhythm or atypical QRS morphology---rather than the
choice of model, which argues for per-subject quality screening at enrolment.

\subsection{Limitations}

The study covers only 10 subjects. ECG-ID contains 90 in all, and the pipeline
reaches them without code changes; the additional databases named in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} (PTB-XL, MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm, CYBHi, LUDB)
would enable the cross-database validation that is the obvious next step.

Every experiment uses a single session per subject, so we cannot yet speak to
intra-subject drift across sessions. The KAN is written from scratch in
PyTorch; the \texttt{efficient-kan} library might give slightly different
numbers. Finally, no augmentation is applied---additive noise or time warping
could help the subjects with the fewest beats.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper reported what we believe is the first evaluation of KAN
autoencoders for one-class ECG biometric verification, set against four
traditional one-class detectors and an MLP autoencoder over two
representations built from WTMM ridge-line analysis of Morlet CWT scalograms.

The signal chain is simple to state: NeuroKit2 cleans the raw ECG,
Pan--Tompkins finds the R-peaks, 700\,ms windows are cut around them, the
Morlet CWT turns each beat into a scalogram, and WTMM chain extraction
condenses that scalogram into 26 numbers. This compact descriptor is aimed
squarely at the small-sample reality (15--30 beats per subject) of
single-session enrolment.

For visualisation we rely on t-SNE, which brings out local cluster structure
and lets us see, before any classifier is trained, that the WTMM feature space
is already discriminative---and, side by side, how much tighter its clusters
are than those of the raw waveform.

The headline quantitative result is twofold: WTMM paired with Isolation Forest
gives the best AUROC overall (0.932, averaged over 10 subjects), and the KAN
autoencoder gives the lowest EER of any model tested (0.113 on WTMM),
beating the MLP autoencoder on this operationally important metric. Both hold
with only 15--30 training beats per subject---the constraint that
single-session ECG enrolment imposes.

A closer study of the KAN's latent representation, its sensitivity to the
B-spline knot count, and its behaviour across all 90 ECG-ID subjects is planned
next.
