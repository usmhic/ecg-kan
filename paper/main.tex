  \textbf{\color{deepblue}Abstract.}\quad
  The electrocardiogram (ECG) encodes each person's cardiac anatomy
  as a time-varying electrical signal that can only be produced by a
  living heart, making it a compelling biometric modality.
  This paper presents a one-class verification system that converts
  individual heartbeats into compact feature representations using
  the Wavelet Transform Modulus Maxima (WTMM) method applied to a
  Morlet continuous wavelet transform (CWT) scalogram.
  Two complementary feature sets are derived: the raw z-scored beat
  waveform (compressed via PCA) and a 26-dimensional WTMM statistical
  summary encoding line count, mean and standard deviation of peak
  frequency, and mean and standard deviation of peak power across
  five log-spaced frequency bands.
  Six one-class classifiers are evaluated per subject in a one-versus-rest
  protocol: One-Class SVM (OC-SVM), Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor
  (LOF), Elliptic Envelope, an MLP autoencoder, and a
  Kolmogorov--Arnold Network (KAN) autoencoder.
  The KAN autoencoder implements learnable B-spline edge functions,
  making it the first reported use of KAN autoencoders for one-class
  ECG biometric verification.
  Experiments are conducted on the ECG-ID database~\cite{lugovaya2005}
  using 10 subjects.
  Code and notebooks are available at
  \url{\repourl}~\cite{hichou2026repo}.\\[4pt]
  \textbf{\color{deepblue}Keywords:}~
  ECG biometrics; wavelet transform modulus maxima; Morlet CWT;
  one-class classification; One-Class SVM; Isolation Forest; LOF;
  Kolmogorov--Arnold networks; B-spline autoencoder; anomaly detection.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Introduction}

Fingerprints, faces, and iris scans are now deployed at scale, but they
share a fundamental vulnerability: each can be captured from surfaces
or photographs and replayed without the subject's knowledge.
The ECG circumvents this problem because it records the electrical
activity of the myocardium, which depends on each person's unique
cardiac geometry, and it can only be generated by a beating heart.
This built-in liveness property has made ECG an active research topic
in biometric verification~\cite{bhuva2023}.

The central engineering challenge is representation.
A raw ECG heartbeat carries information in both the time and frequency
domains simultaneously: slow P and T waves dominated by sub-10 Hz
content and the sharp QRS complex spanning 5--60 Hz~\cite{clifford2006}.
A global spectrum collapses this temporal structure; a plain time-domain
waveform misses the frequency hierarchy.
The Morlet CWT resolves both domains at once and adapts its window width
to the scale, giving better time resolution at high frequencies and
better frequency resolution at low frequencies~\cite{byeon2019}.
The WTMM skeleton~\cite{mallat1992}, computed from the CWT modulus,
then discards redundant energy and retains only the ridge-like structures
that trace the dominant singularities of each heartbeat.

Most published ECG biometric systems are closed-set classifiers trained
on data from all enrolled subjects simultaneously~\cite{elboujnouni2022,
elboujnouni2020}.
Such systems cannot operate at enrolment time, when only one subject's
data is available.
This work frames verification as a \emph{one-class} problem: each model
is trained exclusively on one person's heartbeats and must decide at
test time whether a new beat belongs to that person or to an impostor.

We evaluate six models spanning classical and neural approaches.
Four traditional one-class methods --- OC-SVM~\cite{scholkopf2001},
Isolation Forest~\cite{liu2008isolation}, LOF~\cite{breunig2000lof},
and Elliptic Envelope --- serve as baselines.
An MLP autoencoder provides a deep-learning reference.
The primary novel contribution is a \textbf{KAN autoencoder}~\cite{liu2024},
which replaces every connection in the network with a learnable B-spline
function.
KANs have recently demonstrated competitive performance on cardiac
classification tasks~\cite{jahin2025, zhao2025}, but their application to
one-class anomaly detection for biometrics is, to our knowledge, new.

A key motivation for the KAN is the sparsity of the WTMM feature space:
most values are near zero, with signal concentrated in a small number of
non-zero entries.
Standard MLP activations apply the same function shape everywhere; a
B-spline can concentrate its flexibility exactly where data lies.
Whether this theoretical advantage translates into better verification
performance with 15--30 training beats per subject is the main empirical
question addressed here.

All experiments are fully reproducible via two companion Jupyter
notebooks~\cite{hichou2026repo}: one for the signal-processing pipeline
and one for the classifier benchmark.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{ECG Biometrics}

Israel~et~al.~\cite{israel2005} were among the first to study ECG
identification systematically, extracting PQRST interval and amplitude
features and achieving identification rates above 90\% on small groups.
Pan and Tompkins~\cite{pan1985} provided the foundational real-time QRS
detection algorithm that underpins most subsequent beat-segmentation
pipelines, including the NeuroKit2 implementation~\cite{makowski2021}
used in this work.

Image-based methods became prominent with Byeon~et~al.~\cite{byeon2019},
who converted ECG segments into Morlet scalograms and fed them into deep
convolutional networks, showing that time-frequency images carry
between-subject information invisible in the raw signal.
Ozaltin and Yeniay~\cite{ozaltin2023} combined CWT scalograms with a
CNN-SVM pipeline and reported accuracy up to 99.21\% on ECG-ID, but
on a closed-set identification task rather than one-class verification.

El~Boujnouni~et~al.~\cite{elboujnouni2022} proposed a CWT + DWT +
Capsule Network approach evaluated on four PhysioNet databases including
ECG-ID, achieving up to 100\% identification accuracy.
Their earlier work~\cite{elboujnouni2020} first demonstrated the viability
of ECG scalograms as CapsNet input, motivating the scalogram-to-feature
pipeline adopted here.
Both works, however, operate in the closed-set identification mode and
require data from all classes during training, unlike the open-set
one-class setting studied in this paper.

\subsection{Wavelet Transform Modulus Maxima}

Mallat and Hwang~\cite{mallat1992} introduced WTMM as a framework for
detecting and characterising signal singularities across scales.
The local maxima of the CWT modulus, tracked downward through scales,
converge to singularity locations and their decay rate encodes the
Lipschitz regularity.
WTMM has been applied to ECG denoising and beat
classification~\cite{clifford2006}, but its use as a feature
representation for one-class biometric verification is new.

\subsection{One-Class Classification}

The OC-SVM~\cite{scholkopf2001} finds a maximum-margin hyperplane
separating training data from the origin in kernel space.
Isolation Forest~\cite{liu2008isolation} detects anomalies by building
random trees and measuring how quickly points become isolated.
LOF~\cite{breunig2000lof} computes a local density ratio to score
how well each point fits its neighbourhood.
Autoencoder-based anomaly detection~\cite{ruff2021} trains a
neural network to reconstruct normal data; high reconstruction error
at test time flags anomalies.
This approach has been applied to ECG in clinical
settings~\cite{kiyasseh2021}, but not with a KAN backbone for
biometric verification.

\subsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold Networks}

Liu~et~al.~\cite{liu2024} proposed KANs as a learnable replacement for
standard MLP layers: each edge carries an independent B-spline function
instead of a fixed activation applied per node.
KANs have shown competitive or superior performance on cardiac tasks
including heart disease detection~\cite{jahin2025} and arrhythmia
classification~\cite{zhao2025}.
Their use in one-class settings, trained only on positive examples, has
not been explored before.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

All experiments use the \textbf{ECG-ID database}~\cite{lugovaya2005},
available on PhysioNet~\cite{goldberger2000}.
The database contains 310 single-lead resting ECG recordings from 90
volunteers, sampled at $f_s = 500$\,Hz using ECG lead I.
Each recording lasts approximately 20\,s and yields between 15 and 30
heartbeat cycles.
Some subjects contributed multiple sessions; we use only the first
session per subject, following the protocol of El~Boujnouni~et~al.\
\cite{elboujnouni2022}, which also used the ECG-ID database.
We run experiments on the first 10 subjects, producing 10 independent
one-class classification problems.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Signal Preprocessing}
\label{subsec:preproc}

Let $x[n]$ be the raw ECG sampled at $f_s = 500$\,Hz.
The two dominant noise sources are baseline wander (drift below 0.5\,Hz
from respiration and electrode movement) and high-frequency muscle
artefact above 40\,Hz~\cite{clifford2006,elboujnouni2022}.
In the companion notebooks, cleaning is delegated to NeuroKit2
\cite{makowski2021}, which applies a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth
bandpass filter:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  x_{\text{clean}}[n]
  = \mathcal{F}_{\text{BP}}\!\left\{x[n]\right\},
  \qquad
  f_{\text{low}} = 0.5\,\text{Hz},
  \quad
  f_{\text{high}} = 40\,\text{Hz}.
  \label{eq:bp}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
Applying the filter forward and backward in time (\texttt{filtfilt})
eliminates phase shift, which is critical because a phase-shifted R-peak
would corrupt all downstream window-based features.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{R-Peak Detection and Beat Segmentation}
\label{subsec:beats}

R-peaks are located using NeuroKit2's implementation of the
Pan--Tompkins algorithm~\cite{pan1985, makowski2021}, which applies a
derivative filter, squaring, and a moving-window integrator before
thresholding.
Let $\{r_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ be the detected peak indices.
Each beat $b_k$ is extracted as:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  b_k[m]
  = x_{\text{clean}}\!\left[r_k + m\right],
  \qquad
  m \in \bigl[-T_{\text{pre}},\; T_{\text{post}}\bigr),
  \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
with $T_{\text{pre}} = 125$ samples (250\,ms) and
$T_{\text{post}} = 225$ samples (450\,ms), giving a 700\,ms window of
$N = 350$ samples.
The asymmetric window matches the physiology: the T wave ends roughly
400--450\,ms after the R-peak at resting heart rates, so more time is
allocated on the right side~\cite{clifford2006}.
Beat windows that would fall outside recording boundaries are discarded.
In the notebooks, NeuroKit2's \texttt{epochs\_create} function handles
this extraction, and each beat is z-score normalised to remove amplitude
differences from lead placement~\cite{elboujnouni2022}:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{b}_k[m]
  = \frac{b_k[m] - \mu_{b_k}}{\sigma_{b_k} + \epsilon},
  \qquad \epsilon = 10^{-8}.
  \label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Morlet CWT and Scalogram}
\label{subsec:cwt}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

The ECG is non-stationary: P-wave energy is concentrated below 10\,Hz,
the QRS complex spans 5--60\,Hz, and the T wave again falls below
10\,Hz~\cite{byeon2019, clifford2006}.
A global power spectrum merges these events; a short-time Fourier
transform uses a fixed window at all frequencies.
The CWT uses a scale-adaptive window: narrow at high frequencies (good
temporal precision for the QRS) and wide at low frequencies (good
spectral precision for P and T waves).
This multi-resolution property matches the cardiac cycle well, as
demonstrated empirically by Byeon~et~al.~\cite{byeon2019} and
El~Boujnouni~et~al.~\cite{elboujnouni2022} using Morlet scalograms as
input to deep classifiers.

\subsubsection{CWT Definition}

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \CWT_{b_k}(a,\, \tau)
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
    b_k(t)\;
    \psi^*\!\!\left(\frac{t - \tau}{a}\right) dt,
  \label{eq:cwt}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
where $a > 0$ is the scale, $\tau$ is the time shift, and $1/\sqrt{a}$
normalises energy across scales.
In the notebooks, \texttt{pywt.cwt} computes this on the discrete beat
array.

\subsubsection{Morlet Wavelet}

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\text{Morl}}(t)
  = \pi^{-1/4}\,
    e^{\,i\,\omega_0 t}\,
    e^{-t^2/2},
  \qquad \omega_0 = 6.
  \label{eq:morlet}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
The Gaussian envelope achieves the Heisenberg time-frequency bound
$\Delta t \cdot \Delta \omega \geq \tfrac{1}{2}$, giving the tightest
possible joint localisation.
Setting $\omega_0 = 6$ ensures the admissibility condition
$\int \psi\,dt \approx 0$ holds numerically so the transform is
invertible~\cite{mallat1999book}.
PyWavelets retrieves the central frequency as
\texttt{cf = pywt.central\_frequency('morl')}, and scales are mapped
from a log-spaced frequency grid via:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  a_j = \frac{c_\psi \cdot f_s}{f_j},
  \qquad f_j \in \bigl[\,1,\; 60\,\bigr]\,\text{Hz},
  \quad j = 1,\ldots,80.
  \label{eq:scale_freq}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\subsubsection{Scalogram}

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \Scalo(a,\, \tau)
  = \abs{\CWT_{b_k}(a,\, \tau)}^2.
  \label{eq:scalogram}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
The result is an $80 \times 350$ matrix for each beat.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{WTMM Feature Extraction}
\label{subsec:wtmm}

\subsubsection{Ridge-Line Detection}

The theoretical basis is Mallat and Hwang's singularity
theorem~\cite{mallat1992, mallat1999book}: if $b_k$ has a singularity
of Lipschitz exponent $\alpha$ at time $t_0$, the CWT modulus satisfies
$|\CWT_{b_k}(a,\tau)| \leq A\,a^{\alpha+1/2}$ as $a \to 0$ and
$\tau \to t_0$.
The local maxima of the modulus therefore converge towards singularity
locations as the scale decreases, and their decay encodes $\alpha$.
For the ECG, the sharp QRS onset has small $\alpha$ and produces strong
maxima lines that persist across all 80 scales; the smoother P and T
waves produce weaker, shorter lines.

A local maximum at position $(a, \tau_0)$ satisfies:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial \tau}
  \abs{\CWT_{b_k}(a,\tau)}\bigg|_{\tau=\tau_0} = 0
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \abs{\CWT_{b_k}(a,\tau_0)}
  > \abs{\CWT_{b_k}(a,\tau)}
  \;\;\forall\,\tau \in \mathcal{N}(\tau_0).
  \label{eq:wtmm_def}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
On the discrete $80 \times 350$ grid this reduces to a simple
neighbourhood comparison, implemented in \texttt{compute\_wtmm\_lines}
in the notebook.

\subsubsection{Chain Linking}

Adjacent-scale maxima are connected into chains when:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \abs{\tau_0^{(a+\delta a)} - \tau_0^{(a)}}
  \;\leq\;
  \lambda(a)
  = \max\!\left(1,\;\left\lfloor\tfrac{a}{2}\right\rfloor\right).
  \label{eq:link}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
The tolerance $\lambda(a)$ grows with scale, reflecting the increasing
spatial extent of the wavelet.
Chains shorter than two consecutive scale rows are discarded as noise.

\subsubsection{WTMM Statistical Feature Vector}

Rather than using a fixed-resolution pixel image (which requires
sufficient training samples to cover the image dimensionality), the
notebooks derive a compact 26-dimensional feature vector from the chain
set.
This design choice was motivated by the small number of available beats
per subject (15--30) and follows the philosophy of extracting
task-relevant summary statistics as in~\cite{clifford2006}.

The feature vector is constructed as follows.
Let $L$ be the total number of chains extracted from beat $b_k$.
The frequency axis $[f_{\min}, f_{\max}]$ is divided into
$N_B = 5$ log-spaced bands $[f_\ell^{\text{lo}},\, f_\ell^{\text{hi}}]$,
$\ell = 1,\ldots,5$.
For each band the code collects all chain points whose frequency
$f_j = c_\psi f_s / a_j$ falls within the band.
If the band is non-empty, five statistics are extracted: band line count
$C_\ell$, mean peak frequency $\bar{f}_\ell$, frequency standard deviation
$\sigma_{f,\ell}$, mean peak power $\bar{p}_\ell$, and power standard
deviation $\sigma_{p,\ell}$.
Empty bands contribute five zeros.
The full feature vector is:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}(b_k)
  = \Bigl[
      L,\;
      C_1,\, \bar{f}_1,\, \sigma_{f,1},\, \bar{p}_1,\, \sigma_{p,1},\;
      \ldots,\;
      C_5,\, \bar{f}_5,\, \sigma_{f,5},\, \bar{p}_5,\, \sigma_{p,5}
    \Bigr]
  \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{26}.
  \label{eq:feat_vec}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
All classifiers also receive the z-scored raw waveform
$\tilde{b}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{350}$, reduced to 40 dimensions by PCA
fitted on the training split of each subject.
The PCA dimensionality is clipped to
$\min(n_{\text{train}},\, 350,\, 40)$ to avoid rank-deficiency errors
when a subject has very few beats.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Traditional One-Class Classifiers}
\label{subsec:trad}

\subsubsection{One-Class SVM}

Each feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i$ is mapped to a kernel feature space
via an RBF kernel
$k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \exp(-\gamma\norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'}^2)$
with $\gamma = 1/d$ (\texttt{scale} default, $d$ = feature dimension).
The OC-SVM~\cite{scholkopf2001} solves:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\xi},\rho}
  \;\frac{1}{2}\norm{\mathbf{w}}^2
  - \rho
  + \frac{1}{\nu n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_i
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
  \langle\mathbf{w},\phi(\mathbf{x}_i)\rangle \geq \rho - \xi_i,
  \;\xi_i \geq 0,
  \label{eq:ocsvm}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
with $\nu = 0.1$.
A test beat is accepted if its decision function value is positive.

\subsubsection{Isolation Forest}

The Isolation Forest~\cite{liu2008isolation} builds an ensemble of
200 random trees.
Anomalous points are isolated faster (shorter path lengths), so the
mean path length across trees becomes the anomaly score.
Contamination is set to 0.1 in the notebooks.

\subsubsection{Local Outlier Factor}

LOF~\cite{breunig2000lof} computes the ratio of average local
reachability density in a point's neighbourhood to the densities of
its neighbours.
Scores close to 1.0 indicate a point lies in a region of similar density
to its neighbours (inlier); scores $\gg 1$ indicate an outlier.
The number of neighbours is clamped to
$\min(20,\, n_{\text{train}} - 1)$ to avoid failures when a subject has
fewer beats than the default $k = 20$.

\subsubsection{Elliptic Envelope}

The Elliptic Envelope fits a Gaussian distribution to the training data
and reports the Mahalanobis distance from the centre as the
anomaly score.
It assumes the genuine class is approximately elliptical in feature
space, an assumption that may not hold for high-dimensional raw
waveform features but is reasonable for the 26-dimensional WTMM
summary.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{MLP Autoencoder}
\label{subsec:mlp}

The MLP autoencoder is trained to reconstruct each genuine beat
from a compact bottleneck representation.
At test time, impostor beats produce higher reconstruction error.
The architecture is:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \text{Encoder:}\;
  d \xrightarrow{128} \xrightarrow{64} 16,
  \qquad
  \text{Decoder:}\;
  16 \xrightarrow{64} \xrightarrow{128} d,
  \label{eq:mlp_arch}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
with ReLU activations throughout.
Training uses Adam~\cite{kingma2015} with $\eta = 10^{-3}$, batch
size 32, and 80 epochs.
The anomaly score is the negative mean squared reconstruction error:
$s(\mathbf{x}) = -\norm{\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}}^2$.
The decision threshold is set at the 90th percentile of training errors,
so 10\% of genuine training beats are treated as boundary cases.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{KAN Autoencoder}
\label{subsec:kan}

\subsubsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold Representation}

The Kolmogorov--Arnold theorem states that any continuous function
$f:[0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ can be written as:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  f(x_1,\dots,x_n)
  = \sum_{q=0}^{2n}
    \Phi_q\!\left(
      \sum_{p=1}^{n} \phi_{q,p}(x_p)
    \right),
  \label{eq:ka_theorem}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
where all complexity is expressed through univariate functions.
A KAN~\cite{liu2024} operationalises this by placing a learnable
univariate function on each network edge.

\subsubsection{KAN Layer}

Each edge from neuron $j$ to neuron $i$ carries:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{ij}(x)
  = w_b\;\mathrm{silu}(x)
  + w_s\;\sum_{k} c_{ijk}\, B_k(x),
  \label{eq:kan_edge}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
where $\mathrm{silu}(x) = x/(1+e^{-x})$ is a fixed residual base,
$B_k$ are cubic B-spline basis functions on a uniform grid of $G = 5$
intervals over $[-1, 1]$ (order $k = 3$, giving $G + k = 8$ basis
functions per edge), and $w_b$, $w_s$, $\{c_{ijk}\}$ are learned by
gradient descent.
The grid is clamped to $[-1, 1]$ at forward pass time to avoid
out-of-support extrapolation.
The output of neuron $i$ in layer $\ell+1$ is:
$x_i^{(\ell+1)} = \sum_{j} \phi_{ij}(x_j^{(\ell)})$.

The B-spline's local support means the model concentrates flexibility
where training data actually lies, rather than fitting a global
polynomial.
This is attractive for WTMM features, where most entries are near
zero and information is sparse.

\subsubsection{Architecture and Training}

The KAN autoencoder uses the same encoder--decoder depth as the MLP:

\begin{mathbox}
\begin{equation}
  \text{Encoder:}\;
  d \xrightarrow{\phi^{(1)}} 64 \xrightarrow{\phi^{(2)}} 12,
  \qquad
  \text{Decoder:}\;
  12 \xrightarrow{\phi^{(3)}} 64 \xrightarrow{\phi^{(4)}} d.
  \label{eq:kan_arch}
\end{equation}
\end{mathbox}

\noindent
Each arrow is one \texttt{BSplineEdge} layer, as implemented in the
companion notebook.
Training uses AdamW~\cite{kingma2015} with $\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$,
weight decay $10^{-4}$, gradient norm clipping at 1.0,
cosine annealing learning-rate schedule, batch size 32, and 80 epochs.
The reconstruction loss is mean squared error.
Anomaly scoring and threshold selection follow the same protocol as the
MLP autoencoder.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:eval}

For each of the 10 subjects, 70\,\% of their beats are used for training
and the remaining 30\,\% for testing as genuine samples.
An equal number of beats is sampled uniformly at random from all other
subjects to form the impostor test set, giving a balanced binary test
split.
The random seed varies per subject ($42 + \text{subject\_index}$) to
avoid seed-selection artefacts.

All six classifiers are trained and evaluated independently per subject,
giving 10 one-class problems.
Each experiment is repeated on both feature sets (raw PCA-40 and WTMM-26).
The following metrics are reported:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{AUROC}: Area under the ROC curve.
    Threshold-free separation between genuine and impostor distributions.
    Values: 0.5 (random), 1.0 (perfect).
  \item \textbf{F1 score}: Harmonic mean of precision and recall at the
    training-percentile threshold.
  \item \textbf{EER}: Equal Error Rate --- the operating point where
    false rejection rate equals false acceptance rate.
    Lower is better.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
All metrics are reported as mean and standard deviation across the 10
subjects.
Complete experimental code is available in the companion
notebooks~\cite{hichou2026repo}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{WTMM as a One-Class Feature Representation}

The WTMM statistical summary has two properties that make it suitable for
one-class biometric verification.
First, \emph{within-subject consistency}: the main events of a person's
heartbeat --- QRS onset, T-wave peak --- appear at stable positions across
beats, so the 26-dimensional feature vectors of one subject form a compact
cluster in feature space (Figure~\ref{fig:pca_space}).
Second, \emph{between-subject discriminability}: individual differences in
cardiac anatomy manifest as different chain densities, frequency distributions,
and energy profiles across bands.
Both properties are required simultaneously: a compact genuine class
makes the boundary learnable from the 15--22 training beats available per
subject, while discriminability keeps impostors outside that boundary.
This parallels the motivation of El~Boujnouni~et~al.~\cite{elboujnouni2022},
who observed that Morlet scalogram images carry between-subject information
not visible in the raw signal.

Experimentally (Table~\ref{tab:results}), the WTMM feature set yields
lower mean AUROC than the raw waveform features for most traditional
classifiers, but produces better F1 scores for the MLP and KAN autoencoders.
This suggests that the 26-d WTMM summary compresses away noise while
retaining the structural information that reconstruction-based models need,
whereas the richer raw waveform (after PCA-40 compression) benefits
distance-based methods more.

\subsection{Behaviour of Traditional Classifiers}

On raw PCA-40 features, the MLP autoencoder achieves the highest mean
AUROC (0.967) and the KAN autoencoder the best mean EER (0.085), while the
Elliptic Envelope is a close second on AUROC (0.965), suggesting that
genuine-class beats form a roughly Gaussian cluster in the first 40 PCA
dimensions.
The OC-SVM (AUROC 0.956) performs well but requires tuning of the RBF
$\gamma$ parameter; the \texttt{scale} default ($1/d$) works adequately here
but a per-subject search would likely close the gap.
LOF (AUROC 0.836) lags, being sensitive to the adaptive $k$-clamping at the
small beat counts available per subject.

On WTMM features, Isolation Forest leads (AUROC 0.945), while OC-SVM
(0.912) and Elliptic Envelope (0.926) are closely matched.
The KAN autoencoder (0.889) and MLP (0.908) trail slightly, though the
KAN achieves the best F1 on WTMM features (0.688), suggesting
well-calibrated reconstruction thresholds on the compact feature space.
The Elliptic Envelope's AUROC drops by 0.039 from the raw-feature setting,
confirming that the 26-dimensional WTMM space is less Gaussian than the
PCA-compressed waveform.

\subsection{MLP vs.\ KAN Autoencoder}

On raw features, the MLP autoencoder leads on AUROC (0.967) while the
KAN autoencoder (0.942) closely follows.
The KAN achieves the best mean F1 on raw features (0.761 vs.\ 0.359 for
the MLP), indicating better-calibrated decision boundaries from its
piecewise B-spline edge functions.
On WTMM features the KAN (AUROC 0.889) is outperformed by Isolation
Forest (0.945) and OC-SVM (0.912), but again leads on F1 (0.688).
The KAN's local-support B-splines concentrate model capacity where data
lies, which translates into a better-calibrated reconstruction threshold
at the 90th training-error percentile compared with the MLP's global ReLU
activations --- a difference that becomes most visible in F1 rather than
AUROC.
A more detailed study of the KAN's latent space, number of knots, and
scaling behaviour across more subjects is planned for future work.

scaling behaviour across more subjects is planned for future work.

\subsection{Comparison with Closed-Set Methods}

The one-class verification setting studied here is inherently more difficult
than the closed-set identification studied by El~Boujnouni~et~al.\
\cite{elboujnouni2022, elboujnouni2020}: those systems see all subjects
during training and can use inter-class boundaries.
Our models see only one subject at enrolment.
A direct accuracy comparison is therefore not meaningful, but the two
evaluations are complementary: closed-set identification measures the
ceiling of performance when all data is known, while one-class verification
measures practical deployability at the enrolment stage.

\subsection{Limitations}

The evaluation is limited to 10 subjects.
The ECG-ID database provides 90 subjects; extending to all 90 is
straightforward with the existing code.
A single recording session is used per subject; intra-subject variability
across sessions is not assessed.
The KAN is implemented from scratch in PyTorch; the \texttt{efficient-kan}
library may produce different results if available.
Finally, no data augmentation is applied; augmentation strategies such as
additive noise or time warping could improve performance for subjects with
very few beats.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented the first evaluation of KAN autoencoders for one-class
ECG biometric verification, benchmarked against four traditional one-class
classifiers and an MLP autoencoder on two feature representations derived
from WTMM ridge-line analysis of Morlet CWT scalograms.
The signal-processing pipeline --- NeuroKit2 cleaning, Pan--Tompkins R-peak
detection, 700\,ms beat segmentation, Morlet CWT, and WTMM chain extraction
--- produces compact features that encode the multiscale singularity structure
of each heartbeat.
The compact 26-dimensional WTMM statistical summary is designed for the
small-sample regime (15--30 beats per subject) that is inherent to
single-session ECG enrolment.

The primary contribution is methodological: introducing and evaluating
KAN-based anomaly detection for ECG biometrics and providing a fully
reproducible comparison across six classifiers and two feature sets.
Building on prior work demonstrating the discriminative power of Morlet
scalograms for ECG identification~\cite{byeon2019,elboujnouni2022} and
the potential of KANs on cardiac classification
tasks~\cite{jahin2025,zhao2025}, this work extends the KAN to the more
challenging one-class open-set setting.
Results are summarised in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.
A deeper study of the KAN's latent representation, its sensitivity to
the number of B-spline knots, and its scaling behaviour across all 90
ECG-ID subjects is planned for follow-up work.
